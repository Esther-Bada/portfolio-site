No CS Degree. No Backend Experience. I Shipped a Live Website Anyway, Here's Exactly What That Looked Like.

The first time my deployed site returned a 404 error, I spent three hours convinced I had broken something fundamental. I hadn't. But I also couldn't immediately tell you why, and that gap was the most honest thing about where I actually was.

My background is in Early Childhood Education. I spent years studying how people learn, where systems fail them, and what it takes to close those gaps. When I started building with AI tools, it wasn't to become a developer, it was to find out whether someone like me could build something real and actually get it live.

This is what that process looked like, including the part that nearly stopped me.

The Tools I Used (and Why)

To get the site live, I used five tools. Not because they were the most sophisticated options, but because they connected without requiring me to become a systems engineer before I could ship anything.

  • Claude Code: for building, editing, and debugging directly inside my project
  • VS Code: my development environment
  • Live Server: real-time browser preview while building
  • GitHub: version control and progress tracking
  • Netlify: deployment, hosting, and form handling

The insight that changed how I approached everything: I wasn't choosing tools to learn. I was choosing tools to ship.

The Decisions I Made Before I Started

Netlify removed the need to manage servers. That was the entire reason I chose it — not the feature list, but the problems it made irrelevant.

Netlify Forms handled contact submissions automatically. No backend to build, no database to maintain, no reason to pause and learn a new system before my site was even live.

Claude Code was different from every other tool I used. It didn't just generate code — it helped me understand what my own files were actually doing. I could describe a problem in plain language and see how it connected to the real structure of my project.

Every tool I chose did one specific thing well. I didn't pick tools with the most features. I picked tools that reduced the number of decisions I had to make along the way.

The Honest Cost Breakdown

One thing I tracked carefully was cost, because I think accessibility matters. Here's what building and deploying this site actually cost me:

  • VS Code: free
  • Live Server: free
  • GitHub: free (public repositories)
  • Netlify: free tier (hosting and forms)
  • Claude: paid plan (optional depending on usage)

You can build and deploy a working website with almost no upfront cost. That changes who gets to build things — and that matters.

The Part That Almost Stopped Me

The hardest part of this project was not writing code. It was deployment.

I ran into repeated issues connecting my GitHub repository to Netlify. Even after configuring everything correctly, the deployed site kept returning 404 errors. The manual deployment worked fine. The Git-integrated version didn't. I went through every setting, read the documentation twice, and still couldn't get it to resolve the way it was supposed to.

What I eventually understood: the environment where your site lives does not behave the same way as the environment where you built it. That gap — between local and live — is where most problems actually hide.

Building the site was only half the work. Getting it into the world is where you find out what you actually understand.

What Shifted When It Went Live

Before this, I believed software development was a closed discipline, something that required years of formal preparation before you could produce anything meaningful.

That's not entirely wrong. But it's not the full picture either.

What I found is that the ability to ship depends less on how much syntax you've memorized and more on how well you can think through a problem, stay in the work when things get unclear, and choose the right tool at the right moment.

I can build real systems. I can deploy them. I can debug them when they break. And I did all of that without a traditional engineering background.

What I'd Do Differently

If I were starting over, I'd spend far less time trying to fully understand every tool before touching it, and far more time just building. The understanding came fastest when I was already inside a real problem, not standing outside it waiting to feel prepared.

I'm not trying to become a conventional developer. I'm building things that sit at the intersection of education, technology, and human experience. This site was proof that I can.

If you're waiting until you feel ready: that feeling doesn't arrive before you begin. It arrives somewhere in the middle, usually right after something breaks and you figure out how to fix it. If you're working on something and want to talk it through, reach out — I'd genuinely like to hear about it.
